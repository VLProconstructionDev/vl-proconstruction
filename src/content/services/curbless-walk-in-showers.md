faqs:
  - q: Can you build a curbless shower on a concrete slab?
    a: Yes, and most of the ones we build here are on slab. There are two ways to get the fall you need without a curb — recess the slab by saw-cutting and re-pouring the shower footprint, or build an engineered sloped assembly and bring the whole bathroom floor up to meet it. Which one fits depends on your drain location, the joist or slab depth, and how the doorway threshold lands. We work that out at the walkthrough, because it has to be designed in from the start rather than changed in week two.
  - q: Will a curbless shower flood the bathroom?
    a: Not when it is designed right. Water is controlled by three things — enough fall to the drain across the whole pan, a drain sized and placed for the flow, and glass or a wall positioned to catch spray. A linear drain set at the back wall lets the entire pan slope one way, which is why we use one on most curbless builds. Add a tiled or recessed threshold strip and the entry stays dry even without a curb.
  - q: What is the difference between a walk-in shower and a curbless shower?
    a: A walk-in shower is any shower you step into rather than step over a tub wall — most have a low tiled curb, usually 3 to 5 inches. A curbless shower has no curb at all, so the bathroom floor runs straight into the shower. Every curbless shower is a walk-in; not every walk-in is curbless. Curbless costs more because of the slab or framing work, and it is what you want for wheelchair access, a rollator or walker, or simply a room that reads as one continuous floor.
  - q: How wide does the entry have to be for a wheelchair?
    a: For a fully accessible roll-in shower, ADA guidance is a 60" x 30" minimum interior and a 36" clear opening, with no threshold over 1/2" and that half-inch beveled. Most homeowners are not building to full ADA — they want a shower that works now and still works later — so we size the opening as wide as the room allows and set real blocking in the walls for grab bars, whether or not the bars go in today.
  - q: Do I need a permit for a walk-in shower in Manatee or Sarasota County?
    a: If the shower stays where it is and nothing moves, usually not. Converting a tub to a walk-in shower, adding or relocating a drain, cutting the slab, or altering a wall does need a permit, and a curbless build almost always touches one of those. We pull it as the licensed contractor of record with the county or your city building department and meet the inspections. Condo and deed-restricted communities may also need association approval before work starts.
  - q: How long does a walk-in shower take to build?
    a: A standard walk-in shower in the existing footprint runs about 2 to 3 weeks. A curbless build runs 3 to 4 weeks, because the slab work and the flood test both have to happen before tile. Frameless glass adds 2 to 3 weeks after the tile is finished — it is templated off the finished surfaces, so it cannot be ordered in advance.
  - q: Is a walk-in shower harder to keep clean than a tub?
    a: Easier, in our experience — there is no tub ledge to reach over and no door track to scrub. What decides it is the tile and the glass. Large-format tile means fewer grout lines, and a pan in mosaic or pebble gives you grip without much upkeep. On glass we recommend a factory-applied protective coating; here the water is hard enough that untreated glass spots within a season.

A walk-in shower is the upgrade that changes how a bathroom feels every morning: no tub wall to climb over, no track to scrub, and a room that looks twice its size once the glass goes in. Curbless takes it a step further — the bathroom floor runs straight into the shower, which is what makes it the right build for aging in place. Both live or die on the same thing: the waterproofed, correctly sloped assembly underneath.
