None of the tiles below is a bad product. Every one of them is genuinely beautiful, and we've
installed all five. The problem is that showrooms are dry, lit from above, and no one has ever
washed their hair in one.

A shower is a different environment entirely. It runs wet for twenty minutes and damp for
hours. It gets soap, shampoo, body oil, and Gulf Coast hard water – which here means calcium
that leaves a film on everything and slowly eats anything alkaline-sensitive. It goes from
ambient to 105°F and back, twice a day, for twenty years. And someone stands in it barefoot.

Materials that shrug that off: glazed porcelain, most glazed ceramic, quartzite, and a few
dense granites. Materials that struggle: almost everything else, in ways that show up in year
two rather than week one, which is why so many people find out the expensive way.

## 1. Polished marble – especially Carrara

This is the number one regret, by a wide margin, because it's also the number one Pinterest
board.

Marble is calcium carbonate. Everything acidic etches it – and "acidic" here includes a lot of
shampoo, most bathroom cleaners, and anything citrus. Etching isn't a stain you can scrub out;
it's a dull spot where the polish is chemically gone. Meanwhile the stone is porous enough
that oils go in and stay, so you get darkened patches around the bench and the niche.

Then there's our water. Bradenton and Sarasota run hard, and the calcium film that leaves on
glass also sits on the marble. The usual fix – a mildly acidic descaler – is exactly the thing
that etches the stone. You're picking between a cloudy surface and a damaged one.

And polished marble underfoot in a wet shower is genuinely slippery. Polished stone rarely
clears DCOF 0.42, which is the wet-floor threshold building codes and manufacturers reference.

**What to do instead.** Honed marble on the *walls*, outside the direct spray if you can plan
it, sealed on a schedule you'll actually keep – it patinas rather than failing. Or a
marble-look porcelain, which has come a long way: the good ones have four or five different
vein patterns per box, and from standing height in a shower nobody knows. On the floor,
porcelain mosaic, always.

> Rule of thumb: the stone that makes a beautiful shower is the one you'd also be happy with
> slightly dull. If a matte spot would ruin it for you, don't put it in the spray.

## 2. Large-format tile on the shower floor

24×24 and bigger on the walls: excellent, modern, fewer grout lines, and what most of our
[custom shower](/services/custom-showers) jobs use. The same tile on the shower *floor* is a
geometry problem.

A shower floor has to slope 1/4" per foot to the drain, from four directions if it's a center
drain. Tile is flat and rigid. The only way a rigid plane follows a cone is to break into
small enough pieces to articulate – which is exactly why shower pans have been mosaic for a
hundred years. Force a large tile onto that slope and you get ponding in the middle of every
tile, lippage at every edge, and a floor that never fully dries. Standing water in a warm
bathroom is how you get the pink film and, eventually, the smell people blame on the drain.

You'll also fail the slip number. Large-format tile is typically smoother than mosaic, and
mosaic wins twice: more grout joints mean more texture underfoot.

**What to do instead.** Two options. A 2×2 or smaller mosaic in the pan – it can be the exact
same product line as the wall tile in most collections, so the look carries. Or a **linear
drain** against one wall, which lets the floor slope in one direction only; that geometry
tolerates a larger tile, up to about 12×24, and it's what makes a truly curbless,
single-material shower floor possible.

## 3. Glass tile – mosaic or large

Glass is the most unforgiving tile there is, and the failures are all installation-adjacent,
which makes them nobody's fault and everybody's problem.

It's transparent, so it shows the thinset behind it: every trowel ridge, every void, every
skim of the wrong color. It needs a white polymer-modified thinset specified for glass, and a
flat substrate, and a slower, fussier install. It moves at a different rate than the substrate
with temperature, so in a shower that cycles hot and cold it puts real stress on the bond
line; glass tiles crack or pop off far more often than porcelain. And on cut edges, cheap
glass chips and the chips are sharp.

On a floor, clear glass is slick and glass mosaic is a maintenance job: all those joints in a
wet zone mean grout to keep clean.

**What to do instead.** Use glass where it's a jewel, not a field: a niche back, a single
accent band, a vanity backsplash. Budget for a tile setter who says "glass" without sighing.
If you want the shimmer across a whole wall, glazed ceramic with a glossy face gets you 80% of
the light at a fraction of the risk.

## 4. Cement and encaustic tile

Those beautiful matte, patterned, slightly chalky tiles – Moroccan and Spanish-looking, often
handmade. We love them. Not in a shower.

Cement tile is unglazed and genuinely porous. It absorbs water and everything dissolved in it,
which means shampoo, conditioner, and dye all leave marks. It needs sealing before grouting,
after grouting, and then periodically forever, and the sealer is what stands between the
pattern and a permanent shadow.

It also effloresces: water moving through the tile carries mineral salts to the surface and
leaves a white bloom. In a shower that's wet every day, that cycle never stops. And the color
is pigment in cement, not glaze, so it wears and lightens in traffic.

**What to do instead.** Put it somewhere it can be a floor that gets wiped, not a surface
that gets rained on – a powder room, an entry, a laundry. If you want the pattern in the
shower, **patterned porcelain** does this extremely well now, because the pattern is printed
under a glaze that doesn't care about any of the above. Most of the major lines carry at least
one patterned collection now, and our [tile and natural stone](/services/tile-natural-stone)
crews set them the same way we'd set any porcelain.

## 5. Deep-textured, 3D and fluted tile

Split-face ledgerstone, chunky 3D geometric panels, fluted and reeded tile – all having a
moment, all built to catch light, and all built to catch everything else too.

Every ridge and recess is somewhere soap scum collects and a squeegee can't reach. Within a
year the texture reads gray instead of white. Split-face natural stone is worse: it's porous,
unsealed on the broken face by definition, and the crevices hold water long enough to grow
things.

There's a second problem people don't anticipate: on a wall you lean against or brush past,
aggressive texture is unpleasant, and on anything near a bench it's a scrape hazard.

**What to do instead.** Keep heavy texture out of the wet zone – it's beautiful on a
bathroom feature wall, a vanity wall, or the outside of the shower. Inside, get your interest
from a **light texture or a fabric-look glaze**, from a large-format stone-look with real vein
movement, or from layout: a vertical stack, a herringbone, or a single-color wall with a
contrasting niche does more for a shower than relief does, and you can clean it.

## The thing that actually decides whether a shower lasts

Every failure above is a finish problem, and finish problems are annoying. The failures that
cost real money are behind the tile: an unsealed corner, a pan liner turned up two inches
instead of four, a niche cut into the waterproofing and patched with caulk.

A shower is a waterproofing assembly with tile on it. We build ours as a system – sloped
mortar bed, bonded membrane or sheet waterproofing, flood-tested before a single tile goes on
– which is the part you can't inspect once it's finished and the only part that determines
whether the wall behind it is dry in ten years. It's also the most common corner cut on a
cheap bid, and we listed the rest of them in
[7 bathroom remodel mistakes](/blog/bathroom-remodel-mistakes).

Choose the tile for the room it's actually in. Then make sure the person setting it is
building the part you'll never see.

## FAQs

**Can I use marble in a shower at all?**
Yes – honed rather than polished, on the walls, sealed on schedule, with the understanding
that it will develop a patina. Keep it out of the pan, and clean it with a pH-neutral stone
cleaner only. If that maintenance sounds like a chore, buy the porcelain version and spend the
difference on the glass.

**What's the safest shower floor tile?**
A porcelain mosaic, 2×2 or smaller, in a matte or lightly textured finish with a DCOF of 0.42
or higher. The grout joints do as much for traction as the tile face does.

**Does sealing solve the problem with porous tile?**
It buys time, not immunity. Sealer slows absorption; it doesn't make limestone, travertine or
cement tile non-porous, and in a daily shower it needs reapplying far more often than the
label suggests. Sealing is a maintenance plan, not a fix.

**Is a curbless shower harder on tile choice?**
It narrows the floor options – you want a linear drain and a single-direction slope, which is
also what makes larger floor tile workable. Planning a
[tub-to-shower conversion](/blog/tub-to-shower-conversion-bradenton) curbless has to happen
before anything is demolished, because it changes the slab.

**What about travertine and limestone?**
Same family of problems as marble, plus travertine's natural voids – filled at the factory,
and the fill pops out over time in a wet area. Both are better outside the shower than in it.
