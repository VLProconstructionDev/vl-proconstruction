Almost nobody regrets the color. The regrets we get called about are specific and they are
always the same five: the floor cupped in the second summer, the quote doubled once the slab
was ground, the 12-mil vinyl in a house with three dogs, the matte finish that shows every
footprint, and the doorway where the new floor dies into a strip of oak nobody planned for.

None of that is a taste problem. It's a process problem – the decision gets made in a
showroom, in twenty minutes, off a sample the size of a placemat, and the parts that actually
decide whether you're happy in year five never come up.

So here's the process we walk clients through, in order. Do these five things and the
material choice at the end mostly makes itself.

## 1. Get a number off your slab before you shop

Every house on the Gulf Coast that sits on grade has moisture moving up through the concrete,
all the time. That's not a defect. What ruins floors is choosing a moisture-sensitive product
before anybody knows how much.

Ask for a **calcium chloride test (MVER)** or an **in-situ RH probe** – both give you a figure
you can hold a manufacturer's warranty against. What you don't want is the free version:
taping a square of plastic to the slab overnight and looking for fog. That's a yes/no guess on
a question that has a number.

The number changes what you can even consider. A slab reading comfortably in spec opens up
engineered hardwood. A high reading means either a mitigation membrane in the budget or a
material that doesn't care – which is most porcelain and most vinyl. Either way you now know
before you fall in love with something.

It also protects you from the cheapest quote in the stack. A floor that fails because it went
down over a wet slab isn't a product defect, it's an installation condition, and the warranty
says so in the part nobody reads. We go deeper on why this decides everything in
[why your flooring choice matters](/blog/why-your-flooring-choice-matters).

> If a contractor won't test, or quotes prep as a single line called "floor prep," you don't
> have an estimate. You have a starting price.

## 2. Take the sample home and live with it for a week

Showroom lighting is overhead, bright, and neutral. Your house is none of those things. West
light through a slider at 5pm makes a gray plank read lavender; the warm LEDs most people
install pull an orange undertone out of anything with red in it.

So borrow the biggest sample they'll give you – a full board or a full tile, not a chip – and
put it on the floor. Not on the counter, not held up to a wall. On the floor, where it will
actually be, in the room that matters most.

Then look at it four times:

- **Morning and late afternoon**, unlit, to see what daylight does to the undertone.
- **At night with your lamps on**, which is when you'll mostly see it.
- **Next to the things that aren't changing** – cabinet doors, the stone you're keeping, the
  trim color, the stair treads.
- **From standing height**, walking past it, not crouched over it. That's the real viewing
  distance and it's where wood-look porcelain either convinces or doesn't.

Get two or three samples down at once. A floor you can't decide between two of is a floor
where either answer is fine – and that's useful, because it means you can let the slab, the
budget, or the lead time break the tie instead of agonizing.

One more thing while the sample is down: put a chair leg on it and drag it. You'll learn more
in ten seconds than in an hour of reading the brochure.

## 3. Buy the spec numbers, not the product name

Brand names and collection names tell you nothing. Every category has one number that
predicts how the floor ages, and it's usually not on the display card.

| Material | The number that matters | What to ask for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Luxury vinyl plank | Wear layer, in mils | 20 mil for a busy house; 12 mil is a rental spec |
| Engineered hardwood | Veneer thickness, in mm | 3 mm+ if you ever want to sand it |
| Porcelain tile | PEI rating | PEI 4 for floors; PEI 5 for entries and laundry |
| Natural stone | Absorption + finish | Honed and sealed; know what etches |
| Laminate | AC rating | AC4 minimum, and keep it out of wet rooms |

Two of those deserve a sentence each, because they're where the money quietly disappears.

**Wear layer is the whole story on LVP.** Two planks that look identical on the rack can be
12 mil and 20 mil and $3/sq ft apart, and the thin one is the one that scuffs white under a
barstool inside two years. The category advertises waterproof, which is true and largely
irrelevant – water was never what was going to kill it. Furniture and grit were.

**Veneer thickness is the whole story on engineered wood.** A 0.6 mm wear layer is a
photograph of wood that you can never refinish. A 4 mm wear layer is a floor your kids can
sand and keep. Both get called "engineered hardwood" in the same showroom.

Also ask for a slip rating on anything going in a bathroom, a laundry, or near a pool door.
Wet porcelain with a polished face is genuinely dangerous, and there's a rated alternative in
almost every line.

## 4. Choose the floor you're willing to clean

This is the tip nobody gives and everybody wishes they'd heard. A floor gets photographed
once and swept two thousand times. The finish decides which of those experiences you optimize
for.

**Dark floors show everything.** Dust, pet hair, footprints, the trail from the back door.
Mid-tone floors with visible grain movement hide all of it. If somebody in the house has dark
hair or a light-colored dog, that alone can settle the color.

**Sheen matters more than color.** High-gloss anything is a mistake in a Florida house –
it telegraphs every scuff and every bit of sand tracked in from the beach. Matte and low-satin
finishes are quieter and far more forgiving.

**Heavy texture is a trap.** Deep wire-brushed wood and pronounced-relief tile look
wonderful in a photograph and hold grit in every groove. Light texture is fine; anything you
could lose a crumb in isn't.

**Grout color is a real decision.** Pick a grout close to the tile and the floor reads as one
surface. Pick a contrasting grout and you've drawn a grid on the biggest surface in the house
– and a light grout in a traffic lane will be a different color in three years no matter how
well it's sealed. Epoxy grout costs more and solves most of that.

Ask one question about maintenance before you sign: *what do I clean this with, and what will
void the warranty?* Some finishes are fine with a damp mop and nothing else. Some stone wants
resealing every year or two. None of that is a dealbreaker – it's just a thing to know while
you still have a choice.

## 5. Settle the edges before you sign

The field of the floor is the easy part. Every problem that shows up on the punch list lives
at an edge, and edges are where estimates differ without looking like they do.

Walk the house with whoever's quoting and get an answer at each of these:

- **Every doorway** where the new floor meets an existing one. What's the transition, what's
  it made of, and what color?
- **Every interior door.** A new floor over an old one usually means undercutting doors or
  taking them down and trimming them. Who's doing that?
- **The slider and the front threshold.** Height changes here are the ones you trip on.
- **Baseboard and shoe molding.** Removed and reinstalled, or new? Repainted by whom?
- **Appliance and cabinet toe kicks.** Does the floor run under the fridge and the range, and
  will the dishwasher still come out afterward?
- **Stairs**, if you have them – treads and risers are their own trade and their own number.
- **Attic stock.** Order 10% overage and keep the leftover boxes. Dye lots change, product
  lines get discontinued, and one day somebody will drop something heavy.

Get all of it in writing. An estimate with the edges spelled out is usually the higher one on
the page and almost always the cheaper one in the end, because those items don't disappear –
they just move to a change order after demolition, when you have no leverage and no floor.

## FAQs

**Should the same floor run through the whole house?**
Through the connected living space – entry, living, dining, kitchen, hallways – yes. One
continuous material makes an open plan read as one room instead of five compartments.
Bedrooms behind closed doors and wet rooms can each take their own answer.

**How much extra should I budget for prep?**
On most Gulf Coast houses, plan on removal and disposal at $1–$3/sq ft, flatness work at
$1–$4/sq ft, and moisture mitigation at $2–$5/sq ft if the test calls for it. Getting those
as separate lines is more useful than getting a lower total.

**Is wood-look porcelain worth it over real wood?**
In this climate, often. Large-format planks with a tight, color-matched grout read as wood
from standing height and are immune to the humidity swing that cups real boards. It's most of
what our [tile and natural stone](/services/tile-natural-stone) crews install.

**When should I pick the floor relative to the rest of the remodel?**
Early. The floor sets the height of everything that meets it and the undertone everything else
has to agree with. If a bathroom is in scope, the floor and the
[shower waterproofing](/services/custom-showers) are one decision, not two.

**How long will the install take?**
For 1,500–2,500 sq ft: roughly 3–5 days for vinyl, 5–10 for tile, 4–8 for engineered wood plus
acclimation. Prep adds 1–4 days ahead of all of it.

## The short version

Test the slab, take the sample home, buy the number instead of the name, pick the finish you
can live with dirty, and nail down every edge before you sign. Do that and the last step –
which color – turns out to be the easy one, with three right answers instead of one.
