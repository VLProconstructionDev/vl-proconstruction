Ask ten designers for the golden rule of bathroom layout and you'll get ten answers — the wet
zone rule, the triangle, the 60-inch rule, the "never face the toilet" rule. They're all
describing the same thing from different angles.

Here it is in one line: **the room should read dry to wet as you walk in — vanity first, toilet
out of the sightline, shower or tub at the far end — and every fixture should stay on as few
plumbing walls as possible.**

The first half is what makes a bathroom feel good. The second half is what makes it affordable.
A layout that gets both is why one 5×8 bathroom feels generous and the identical 5×8 next door
feels like a closet with a toilet in it.

| The rule | What it means on a plan | What it costs to ignore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dry to wet, door to back wall | Vanity near the door, wet zone farthest from it | Water tracked across the whole floor |
| Toilet out of the door's sightline | Toilet on the same wall as the door, or screened | The first thing anyone sees, every time |
| Fixtures on one or two plumbing walls | Shared supply and drain runs | $1,500 – $5,000 per fixture moved on slab |
| 30" of clear floor in front of each fixture | Nothing crowds the elbows | A room that's legal and still unusable |
| Door swings away from fixtures | Or use a pocket door | Loses 9 sq ft of usable floor |

## The rule, stated properly

Walk through the door of a bathroom that works and you'll notice the sequence without naming
it. The vanity is right there — that's the fixture you use ten times a day for thirty seconds.
Beyond it, the toilet, usually against the same wall as the door so you're not looking at it.
At the back, farthest from where you entered, the wet zone: the shower, the tub, or both.

That sequence isn't decoration. It sorts the room by three things at once:

1. **How often you use the fixture.** The one you touch most is the one you should reach first.
2. **How wet it gets.** Keeping water at the far end means it isn't tracked across the room, and
   the waterproofing can be concentrated where it belongs.
3. **How private it is.** Least private at the door, most private at the back.

The second half of the rule is structural. Every fixture needs supply lines and a drain, and on
the slab-on-grade construction that most of Manatee and Sarasota county is built on, a drain line
lives in concrete. Keep the toilet, the vanity, and the shower gathered around one or two walls
and they share a run. Scatter them to three walls because it looked better on a sketch and you've
bought yourself a saw-cut trench across the floor.

## What you see from the doorway decides the room

If you only take one thing from this page, take the sightline.

Stand in the doorway of the bathroom you're about to remodel and look straight ahead. Whatever
is on that axis is what the room is, permanently — you'll see it every day, and so will every
guest walking past an open door. If it's a toilet, the room is a toilet. If it's a window, a
tiled wall, a nice vanity, or the glass of a shower, the room is a bathroom.

This is why the toilet almost always wants to sit on the same wall as the door, or tucked behind
the swing. It's also why, in a longer bathroom, the best thing you can put on the far axis is
something with depth — a walk-in shower with clear glass makes a narrow room read all the way to
the back wall, where a shower curtain stops the eye dead at six feet.

> **Test it before demo.** Tape the fixture footprints on the floor with painter's tape, then
> stand in the doorway. Thirty seconds of that catches more layout mistakes than an hour of
> looking at a plan, because a plan is drawn from above and you will never once see this room
> from above.

## The clearances that actually enforce it

The rule is a principle; these are the numbers that decide whether it fits. Two sets matter —
the code minimum you must meet to pass inspection, and the NKBA guideline that's the difference
between passing and being comfortable.

| Clearance | Code minimum | Comfortable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toilet centerline to any wall or fixture | 15" | 18" |
| Clear space in front of the toilet | 21" | 30" |
| Clear space in front of the vanity | 21" | 30" |
| Shower interior | 30" × 30" | 36" × 36" or larger |
| Clear space at the shower entry | 24" | 30" |
| Door opening width | 24" (32" for accessible) | 32" |

Two of those do most of the damage when they're missed. The 15-inch toilet centerline is the one
people lose by an inch when a vanity grows from 30 to 36 inches wide — the drawing works, the
installed room doesn't, and the fix is moving a closet flange in concrete. And the 21 inches in
front of a fixture is a minimum that feels like a minimum; at 30 you can bend over to dry your
feet, and at 21 you can't.

Worth saying plainly: meeting code is not the goal. A bathroom can pass every inspection in
Manatee County and still be a room you dislike using. The guideline numbers are what the room
feels like.

## The plumbing wall is the budget

Here's the part nobody wants to hear. On a slab, the layout question and the budget question are
the same question.

Keep the toilet, vanity, and shower drain where they are and you're remodeling a bathroom. Move
the toilet across the room and you're saw-cutting concrete, trenching to the existing stack,
re-tying the drain at the right fall, patching, and re-pouring — before a single tile goes down.
Rough ranges for what that costs on the Gulf Coast:

| Change to the layout | Typical added cost |
| --- | --- |
| Vanity moved or resized on the same wall | $150 – $600 |
| New vanity on a different wall (supply + drain in a stud wall) | $900 – $2,000 |
| Toilet relocated, slab cut, under 4 ft | $1,200 – $2,800 |
| Toilet relocated across the room | $2,500 – $5,000 |
| Shower drain moved / converted to linear | $1,500 – $3,500 |
| Full layout flip — all three fixtures new positions | $6,000 – $12,000 |
| Doorway widened or converted to a pocket door | $900 – $2,200 |

That last row is the sleeper. In a small bathroom, an inswing door sterilizes roughly nine
square feet of floor that can hold nothing — and in a 40 sq ft bathroom, nine feet is a quarter
of the room. A pocket door, or simply rehanging the door to swing out into the hall, is often the
single highest-value layout change available, and it's the cheapest one on the list.

## Applying it at the three sizes we see most

**The 5×8 (40 sq ft) — the standard Florida hall bath.** One long wall carries everything. Tub or
shower at the far end across the 5-foot dimension, toilet in the middle, vanity by the door. This
is the layout the builder used, it's already correct, and the upgrade is almost never to move
things — it's a shower instead of a tub, a floating vanity so you see floor under it, and a
frameless glass panel instead of a curtain.

**The 5×10 or 6×9 — the stretched hall bath.** Same sequence with room to breathe. This is where
a 36" shower becomes a 48" one, and where the door swing is worth solving.

**The primary bath, 8×10 and up.** Now the rule matters more, not less, because you have enough
room to break it. The wet zone goes to the back — often shower and tub sharing one wet wall, which
also concentrates the waterproofing — the vanity or double vanity takes the long wall near the
door, and the toilet gets screened by a half wall or its own compartment. What you don't do is put
a fixture on each of the four walls because there's space for it. You'll pay for four plumbing
runs and get a room that feels like a corridor.

> **The one move that transforms a small bathroom** isn't a layout change at all — it's removing
> the curb. A [curbless shower](/services/curbless-walk-in-showers) with clear glass makes a 5×8
> read as one continuous floor instead of two boxes. It has to be planned before demo, because on
> slab it means a recessed pour.

## Where the rule bends

- **Powder rooms.** No wet zone, so the sequence collapses. The only thing left is the sightline —
  and in a powder room it's the whole design, because the toilet is 60% of the room. Put the
  toilet behind the door swing and a good mirror or a papered wall on the axis.
- **Windows.** A window on the back wall is worth more than textbook fixture order. Work around
  it rather than tiling over it — natural light and an operable window in a Florida bathroom does
  real work for humidity, on top of the exhaust fan you still need.
- **Aging in place.** If the plan is to stay in the house, the wet zone gets bigger and the door
  gets wider, and the 30" clearances become non-negotiable rather than nice. That's a conversation
  to have at the drawing stage, not at the tile stage.
- **A truly bad original plan.** Sometimes the builder did put the toilet on the door axis with a
  cramped corner shower and there's no fixing it in place. Then you pay to move plumbing — just
  do it knowing that's what you're buying, with the numbers above in front of you.

## FAQs

**What is the golden rule of bathroom design?**
Dry to wet, front to back: vanity nearest the door, toilet out of the sightline, shower or tub at
the far end — with fixtures grouped on as few plumbing walls as possible. It's one rule about how
the room feels and one about what it costs, and good bathrooms satisfy both.

**Where should the toilet go in a bathroom?**
Ideally on the same wall as the door or behind the door swing, so it isn't the first thing you
see. It needs at least 15 inches from its centerline to any wall, vanity, or tub (18 is
comfortable) and 21 inches of clear floor in front (30 is comfortable).

**Should the shower be next to the toilet?**
It's fine and it's common — grouping the toilet and shower keeps the drains together, which is
exactly what you want on a slab. Just protect the 15-inch side clearance at the toilet and make
sure the shower door or panel doesn't swing into it.

**What is the minimum size for a full bathroom?**
Roughly 5×8 (40 sq ft) is the practical floor for a full bath with a tub, and that's the standard
Florida hall bathroom. A three-quarter bath with a shower can work at about 30–35 sq ft. Below
that you're in powder-room territory.

**Can I move the toilet in a bathroom remodel?**
Yes, but on a concrete slab it means saw-cutting the floor, trenching to the drain line, and
re-pouring — typically $1,200–$2,800 for a short move and up to $5,000 across the room. In a
raised-floor or second-story bathroom it's much cheaper because the drain runs in the joist bay.

**Does the vanity have to go by the door?**
No rule is absolute, but it's where it belongs in almost every small bathroom: it's the fixture
you use most, it's dry, and it's the best thing to see from the doorway. In a large primary bath
a double vanity usually takes the long wall, which does the same job.
