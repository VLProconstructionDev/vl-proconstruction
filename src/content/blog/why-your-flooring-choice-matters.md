Almost every flooring conversation we have starts in the wrong place: someone has a photo or a
sample board from a showroom, and the question is which one looks better.

It's a fair question and the least important one. A 2,000 sq ft house has 2,000 sq ft of floor
and maybe 1,600 sq ft of wall you actually see. The floor is the biggest thing in the room, it
runs uninterrupted through spaces the walls divide, and every other decision in the house
negotiates with it — not the other way around.

It's also the one finish that's genuinely hard to undo. Paint is a weekend. A backsplash is a
day. Pulling a failed floor out of a lived-in house means emptying every room, demolition,
dust, and a week of not living there — and if it failed because of what's under it, you pay
for the prep you skipped the first time, plus the removal.

Here's what should actually drive the decision.

## The floor decides how big your house feels

Walk through a house with tile in the entry, carpet in the bedrooms, laminate in the living
room, and a metal strip at every doorway. It reads as a series of small compartments, because
every strip is a visual stop and your eye treats it like a wall.

Now run one floor through the whole main living space. Nothing else changed. The house feels
noticeably larger, and the open plan finally does what it was drawn to do. That's the
highest-leverage thing a floor does, and it's why we push people toward one continuous
material even when it means a plainer one to stay on budget. A quiet floor running everywhere
beats a beautiful floor that stops in three doorways.

Two format details do most of the remaining work:

- **Run direction.** Plank runs the long dimension of the space, or toward the main light
  source. Running it the short way makes a long room read choppy.
- **Scale.** Wide plank (7"+) and large-format tile mean fewer lines across the floor, which
  reads calmer and larger. Small tile and narrow strip draw a grid on the biggest surface in
  the room.

We wrote about this in [what makes a home feel outdated](/blog/what-makes-a-home-feel-outdated-2026):
of the six surfaces that date a house, the floor is the one people fix last and should fix
first.

## In Florida, flooring is a moisture decision before it's a style decision

This is the part that gets skipped, and it's the part that fails. Nearly every house here is
slab-on-grade: your floor sits on a permanently damp mass of concrete and moisture moves up
through it continuously. That's normal. What's not normal is installing a moisture-sensitive
floor over a slab nobody tested.

**Testing means a number, not a look.** A calcium chloride test (MVER) or an in-situ RH probe
gives you a figure you can hold a manufacturer's spec against. Taping plastic down overnight
and looking for fog is a guess — and it's the one most commonly offered.

**The seasonal swing is the other half.** The Gulf Coast runs 60–70% relative humidity most
of the year, then a house gets closed up and conditioned to 45% for a season while the owners
are north. Wood moves with moisture, not temperature — that cycle is what cups boards and
opens gaps, and it's why acclimation time and expansion gaps matter more here than almost
anywhere.

**Salt air on the keys.** On Siesta Key, Longboat, and Anna Maria, fasteners corrode and
anything ferrous in a wet area has a short life.

**Storm-season slabs.** After a wet season, plenty of slabs here are still giving up water
they took on months ago, and installing over one on schedule is how a floor delaminates in
year one. The right answer is sometimes "wait three weeks," which is not a thing a crew paid
by the job likes to say.

> them on your house — it'll find out about them there.

## What each material actually gets you

| Material | Works over a damp slab | Real weakness | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Porcelain / ceramic tile | Yes — the safest choice here | Hard, cold, grout needs sealing | Whole-house, wet areas, lanai |
| Natural stone | Yes, with the right setting bed | Etches and stains; needs sealing | Feature spaces, showers, entries |
| Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) | Yes, most products | Dents under point loads; sun fade | Rentals, budget whole-house, kids & dogs |
| Engineered hardwood | Only with a tested slab + vapor barrier | Refinishable once, maybe twice | Living areas in a conditioned home |
| Solid hardwood | No — not over slab, not here | Moves seasonally; can't take water | Second floors, raised subfloor only |
| Laminate | Marginal | Swells permanently if it wets | Tight budgets, low-traffic bedrooms |

A few honest notes on that table.

**Wood-look porcelain is better than it used to be.** Laid large-format with a small offset
and a grout matched to the tile, it reads as wood from standing height and is functionally
indestructible here — the most practical whole-house floor on the Gulf Coast, and much of what
our [tile and natural stone](/services/tile-natural-stone) work is.

**LVP's problem isn't water, it's furniture.** The category sells on waterproof, which is
true. What you don't get told is that most LVP dents under a piano leg or a heavy barstool,
and a west-facing slider will fade a strip of it. Buy on wear layer thickness — 20 mil or
better for a busy house.

**Engineered hardwood is the floor in the photo above,** and it's doable here — over a tested
slab, with the right vapor retarder and adhesive, and a wear layer thick enough to sand once.
Ask what the slab tested at and what the wear layer measures in millimeters. If neither number
is in the quote, the quote isn't finished.

**Solid hardwood over a slab is a no.** The sleeper-and-subfloor version works and costs more
than people expect. Engineered exists for exactly this reason.

## Most of the job is under the floor

Here's what separates two estimates for what looks like the same floor.

**Flatness.** Large-format tile and wide plank telegraph every dip in a slab — flat to 1/4" in
10 ft is fine under carpet and visibly wrong under 48" tile, where the same dip is lippage at
every joint you walk over barefoot. Fixing it means grinding the highs and pouring
self-leveler in the lows, and on a 1990s house that's a real line item.

**Moisture mitigation.** If the slab tests high, the fix is a roll- or trowel-applied membrane
over the slab. Not cheap, not optional — the alternative is a floor that fails outside
of warranty, because that's an installation condition, not a product defect.

**Transitions and heights.** Every doorway where the new floor meets an old one has to land
somewhere, and so does the bottom of every door, the toe kick, and the slider threshold. Good
estimates say what happens at each one.

## Where the money actually goes

Installed ranges for this market — material plus labor, before prep:

| Floor | Installed / sq ft | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laminate | $4 – $7 | Cheapest today, shortest life here |
| LVP | $5 – $10 | Price tracks wear layer more than looks |
| Porcelain tile | $10 – $20 | Large format and patterns cost more to set |
| Engineered hardwood | $12 – $22 | Wear layer and species drive the spread |
| Natural stone | $15 – $30+ | Material-dependent; setting bed matters |

Prep is separate, and it's where estimates diverge — **removal and disposal** ($1–$3/sq ft,
more for mortar-set tile), **flatness work** ($1–$4/sq ft), and **moisture mitigation**
($2–$5/sq ft if the test says you need it).

So the useful way to read a quote isn't the total — it's whether those three lines exist at
all. A number $4/sq ft under everyone else's usually isn't a better price on the same job.
It's the same material with the prep left out, and you pay for prep either way, just later and
with a floor on top of it.

## Choose by room, not by house

One continuous floor through the connected living space — entry, living, dining, kitchen,
hallways. That's the rule, and it's worth protecting with your budget. Everywhere else, decide
on the room's own terms:

1. **Bedrooms** can break the rule — a softer surface behind a closed door costs nothing
   visually.
2. **Bathrooms** want tile or stone, full stop: a wet room has no upside to a wood product —
   see [the bathroom mistakes that cost the most](/blog/bathroom-remodel-mistakes).
3. **Laundry and the garage entry** take the most abuse and the most standing water. Tile.
4. **Lanai and pool deck** need exterior-rated, slip-rated tile. Interior porcelain on a wet
   pool surround is a liability, not a look.

One thing to settle up front: if a bathroom is in the same scope, the floor and the shower are
one waterproofing decision, not two — and a curbless shower has to be designed into the slab
before anything gets laid.

## FAQs

**Is waterproof LVP really waterproof?**
The plank is; the installation isn't necessarily. Water through a seam sits on the slab under
the floor, which in this climate is where mold starts. LVP handles a spilled glass beautifully
and a running supply line no better than anything else.

**Can I put hardwood over a concrete slab in Florida?**
Engineered, yes — over a slab that's been tested, with the vapor retarder and adhesive the
manufacturer specifies. Solid hardwood, no, not directly. The difference is construction: a
plywood-core engineered board is far more dimensionally stable through our humidity swing.

**How long does a whole-house floor take?**
For 1,500–2,500 sq ft: 3–5 days for LVP or laminate, 5–10 days for tile, 4–8 days for
engineered wood plus acclimation before it starts. Prep — removal, grinding, leveler cure —
adds 1–4 days ahead of all of it.

**What's the most important question to ask a flooring contractor?**
"What did the slab test at, and what's in this price for prep?" An answer with numbers tells
you most of what you need to know about the rest of the job.

Pick the material last. Start with what the slab is doing, decide the format and the run, get
the prep written into the estimate, then go look at samples — at which point several of them
