The expensive part of a bathroom remodel is never the part you shopped for. Nobody gets
wrecked by the faucet. People get wrecked by a membrane that was never installed, a drain
that couldn't move where the drawing said it would, and a permit nobody pulled.

We open up bathrooms across Bradenton, Lakewood Ranch, and Sarasota every week, and the
failures rhyme. Here are the seven, and the question that catches each one while it's still
free to fix.

| The mistake | What it costs to fix later |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Treating waterproofing as an upgrade | $8,000 – $25,000+ — full shower rebuild |
| 2. Designing before locating the plumbing | $2,000 – $6,000, or a compromised design |
| 3. Skipping the permit | Fines, forced demo, a problem at resale |
| 4. Ordering material late — or short | 2–8 weeks stalled; a dye-lot mismatch |
| 5. Budgeting with zero contingency | The job stops where the money stops |
| 6. The wrong tile for a wet Florida room | $4,000 – $12,000 to tear out and reset |
| 7. Hiring on price per square foot | Everything above, at once |

## 1. Treating waterproofing as an upgrade

This one costs the most and shows up the latest.

A tiled shower is not waterproof. Tile and grout are porous — water passes through them by
design. The only thing between your shower and the framing behind it is the waterproofing
assembly: a bonded sheet or liquid-applied membrane over the right substrate, sealed at every
corner, seam, and pipe penetration.

When that layer is missing or interrupted at a corner, nothing happens for a while. Then,
around year three, grout darkens at the base, a tile sounds hollow, or a baseboard in the next
room swells. By then it isn't a repair — it's a demolition that often takes the subfloor and
the adjacent [flooring](/services/flooring) with it.

On our [custom shower](/services/custom-showers) work we use Schluter systems — Kerdi board

> **Ask for it by name.** "We waterproof it" is not an answer. Someone who works in wet areas
> weekly names the brand, the substrate, and the test without pausing.

## 2. Designing the layout before anyone located the plumbing

The second most expensive drawing in a remodel is the pretty one made without opening a wall.

Moving a toilet isn't a design decision — it's a drainage decision. A toilet needs a 3-inch
drain with the right fall and a vent within code distance, and on slab-on-grade construction
(most of this market) that means saw-cutting the slab, trenching, re-plumbing, and re-pouring.
Sinks and showers are forgiving. Toilets are not.

The same applies to the shower drain. In a
[tub-to-shower conversion](/blog/tub-to-shower-conversion-bradenton), the tub drain sits at one
end of the alcove and a shower usually needs it centered, or swapped for a linear drain — and a
curbless entry means recessing the framing or slab so the floor sits flush.

Decide what the room needs to do, have someone look at where the waste lines and vents actually
run, *then* draw the layout. The other order produces a design you pay a premium for or quietly
give up on halfway through.

## 3. Skipping the permit

In Manatee and Sarasota County, a cosmetic swap — same fixtures, same locations — generally
doesn't need a permit. Almost anything past that does:

- **Moving or adding plumbing** → plumbing permit
- **New circuits, GFCI work, lighting, or an exhaust fan** → electrical permit
- **Removing or altering a wall** → building permit, possibly structural review
- **Condos** → association approval on top of the county's

The cost isn't the fine. Unpermitted work gets disclosed when you sell, and a buyer's inspector
who finds a re-plumbed bathroom with no record behind it either kills the deal or extracts a
credit that dwarfs the permit fee. Worse: a water-damage claim traced to unpermitted work is a
claim you may not win. Your contractor should pull it and hand you the card.

## 4. Ordering material late — or short

Tile is made in dye lots. Two boxes of the same SKU from different lots won't match, and you
will see it under bathroom lighting. Two rules fix nearly every material problem:

1. **Order 10–15% overage** — more for diagonal or herringbone layouts, large-format tile, or a
   room with lots of cuts. Breakage and cuts aren't waste; they're part of the quantity.
2. **Have everything on site before demolition.** Not ordered — on site, unboxed, and checked
   for damage and lot consistency.

Custom vanities, imported stone, and frameless glass are the usual schedule killers. Glass can't
even be measured until the tile is set and cured, so it lands 2–3 weeks *after* tile. That's
normal — you should hear it in week one, not week five.

> **The overage is insurance.** Keep the leftover box. A cracked tile five years from now is a
> twenty-minute repair with a match, and a whole-wall problem without one.

## 5. Budgeting with no contingency

Nobody knows what's behind your walls — not you, not us, not the inspector. Around here the
surprises are rot behind an old tub surround, undersized supply lines in 1970s–80s homes,
out-of-level floors that large-format tile will expose, and original cast-iron waste lines
that are far cheaper to replace while the wall is already open.

Set aside **10–20% of the project cost** and treat it as spent. If it isn't needed, it's a
bonus. If it is needed and you didn't set it aside, the job stops mid-stream — and a bathroom
stalled at the substrate stage is a room you can't use in a house you live in.

## 6. The wrong tile for a wet Florida room

| Choice | Where it goes wrong | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polished porcelain on a shower floor | Slip hazard when wet and soapy | Mosaic or textured tile, DCOF ≥ 0.42 |
| Unsealed natural stone | Marble and travertine etch and stain | Seal on schedule; know the upkeep |
| Very large format on wavy walls | Lippage — edges catch the light | Flatten the substrate, or size down |
| Dark grout on light stone | Pigment can stain porous stone | Test on a spare piece first |

The two we get called about most: a polished floor tile in a walk-in shower — beautiful, and a
real hazard for anyone with mobility concerns — and unsealed marble in a high-use bathroom,
where hard water and shampoo work fast. Neither material is wrong; both need choosing with
open eyes. That's most of what we discuss on
[tile and natural stone](/services/tile-natural-stone) jobs before anyone picks a color.

## 7. Hiring on price per square foot

Every mistake above is downstream of this one. Three bids that differ by 30% are almost never
quoting the same job, and the gap is always in what you can't see: waterproofing, substrate
prep, demolition and haul-away, permit fees, and whether the crew is the contractor's own.

Line the bids up item by item. The cheapest one usually turns out to be missing prep,
waterproofing, or the permit — so it isn't cheaper, it's quoting less of your project. The same
pattern shows up in
[flooring quotes across Sarasota](/blog/hard-surface-flooring-companies-sarasota).

## The five questions that prevent all seven

1. **What waterproofing system do you use, and do you flood-test before tile?**
2. **Have you checked where my existing drain and vent lines run before drawing this layout?**
3. **Which permits does this scope need, and are you pulling them?**
4. **Is everything on site before demolition, and how much overage did you order?**
5. **What's your written workmanship warranty, and who is physically doing the work?**

## FAQs

**How long does a bathroom remodel take?**
Most single-shower projects run 1–2 weeks. A full bathroom typically runs 2–4 weeks, plus 2–3
weeks after tile for custom frameless glass.

**Do I need a permit to remodel a bathroom in Manatee County?**
Replacing finishes in the same locations, usually not. Moving a drain or valve, adding circuits
or a fan, or altering a wall, yes — and your contractor should pull it. Condo owners also need
association approval.

**Can you build a curbless shower on a concrete slab?**
Yes, and it's common here. It needs a recessed pour or an engineered sloped assembly with a
linear drain, so it has to be designed in from the start — not changed in week two.

**What's the single most common mistake you see?**
Waterproofing done cheap. It's invisible on handover day, the most expensive thing here to fix,
and the one thing you can't inspect yourself — which is exactly why you ask before you sign.
